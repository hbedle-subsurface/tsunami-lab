# Race the Wave

An interactive tsunami lab for introductory students: tsunamis from earthquakes, volcanoes and asteroids, and how people get out of the way.

I built this lab for my online gen ed course on volcanoes and earthquakes at the University of Oklahoma. It is the third in a set with [Find the Earthquake](https://hbedle-subsurface.github.io/earthquake-lab/) and [Read the Volcano](https://hbedle-subsurface.github.io/volcano-lab/), and it ties them together: the Cascadia megathrust from the earthquake lab and Krakatau from the volcano lab both come back here as tsunami sources.

**Open the lab:** https://hbedle-subsurface.github.io/tsunami-lab/

## What students do

1. **Tsunami speed** - a depth slider shows speed = square root of (g x depth), compared with a jet, a car and a runner.
2. **Growing near shore** - move a wave from the deep ocean onto the shelf and watch it slow, shorten and grow (Green's law).
3. **Near and far** - follow the January 26, 1700 Cascadia tsunami to Seaside, Oregon (minutes) and across the Pacific to Japan (hours), and see why the earthquake itself is the warning on the Cascadia coast.
4. **Volcano tsunamis** - Krakatau 1883, and a race between the air-pressure wave and the classic tsunami from the 2022 Hunga Tonga eruption.
5. **Asteroid tsunamis** - kinetic energy of a stony asteroid by size and speed, compared with the Hiroshima bomb and a Chicxulub-size impact.
6. **Your turn** - each student gets their own evacuation scenario (walking speed, tsunami arrival time, distance to high ground and to a vertical evacuation tower) and decides how to get out in time.

When all six steps are done, the lab makes a certificate with a verification code that students upload to the course site. Most students need about an hour to an hour and a half.

## A note on the models

Travel times use one average depth per route, and wave growth uses Green's law. Both are simple models chosen to show the physics, and the panels say so. Real tsunamis are shaped by the full seafloor and coastline. The evacuation scenarios are made up for each student, using the 10-minute start-up delay from Oregon's evacuation planning.
